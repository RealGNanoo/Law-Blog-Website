**BRUSSELS** — The continued immobilization of roughly $300 billion in Central Bank of Russia foreign exchange reserves across G7 jurisdictions—chiefly held in the European clearinghouse Euroclear—has shifted international legal debate from temporary asset freezing to permanent confiscation. While political pressure to repurpose sovereign capital to fund Ukrainian reconstruction and military aid has intensified, the proposed statutory mechanisms face substantial doctrinal barriers under public international law.

The central controversy divides into two distinct international legal inquiries: whether non-injured third states possess the right under the law of state responsibility to take countermeasures that permanently extinguish property rights, and whether central bank reserves enjoy absolute immunity from execution under customary international law.

---

## 1. The Legal Demarcation: Asset Freezing vs. Expropriation

Under international law, an asset freeze constitutes a temporary suspension of an obligation to release funds or permit foreign exchange transactions. Conversely, confiscation permanently transfers title and ownership away from the sovereign holder.

This distinction is fundamental to the regime of countermeasures codified in the International Law Commission’s Articles on State Responsibility (ARSIWA):

* **The Requirement of Reversibility (Article 49(2) and (3)):** Countermeasures are strictly functional: their sole lawful purpose is to induce a wrongdoing state to cease its internationally wrongful act and provide reparations. Under Article 49(3), countermeasures must be taken in such a way as to permit the resumption of performance of the obligations in question. Once foreign reserves are seized, transferred, and expended on defense or civil reconstruction, the status quo ante cannot be restored, breaching the customary condition of reversibility.
* **Proportionality (Article 51):** While the total material damage caused by unlawful aggression may exceed the sum of immobilized reserves, proportionality under ARSIWA does not merely compare aggregate dollar values. It assesses whether the coercive severity of the countermeasure is commensurate with the gravity of the breach and the rights in question.

Proponents of confiscation argue that where an internationally wrongful act causes catastrophic damage that the responsible state refuses to compensate, transferring sovereign funds constitutes a lawful exercise of substitute performance. However, traditional doctrine holds that countermeasures cannot operate as punitive sanctions or self-executing judicial judgments.

## 2. Standing of Non-Injured States: ARSIWA Article 48 vs. Article 54

A second procedural obstacle is whether third-party states—those not directly attacked—have the legal capacity to execute countermeasures.

The ARSIWA framework distinguishes between directly injured states and third-party states:

| Statutory Parameter | Directly Injured State (ARSIWA Article 42) | Non-Injured Third State (ARSIWA Article 48 / 54) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Invocation of Responsibility** | Full entitlement to invoke responsibility and demand restitution, compensation, and satisfaction. | May claim cessation of the breach and performance of the obligation of reparation in the interest of the injured state. |
| **Right to Take Countermeasures** | Expressly permitted under Articles 49–53 to induce compliance with international obligations. | **Ambiguous / Unsettled:** Article 54 operates as a "savings clause," allowing only "lawful measures," leaving third-party countermeasures uncodified in customary law. |

During the drafting of ARSIWA, state practice was insufficient to establish a customary right for non-injured states to adopt coercive countermeasures, leading the ILC to draft Article 54 as an open-ended placeholder. 

States seeking to repurpose reserves assert that breaches of peremptory norms (*jus cogens*)—such as the prohibition of aggression under Article 2(4) of the UN Charter—generate obligations *erga omnes*, permitting collective economic countermeasures. Yet critics emphasize that state practice remains concentrated among a small coalition of Western jurisdictions, falling short of the uniform and widespread state practice accompanied by *opinio juris* required to establish a new customary exception.

---

## 3. Central Bank Immunity from Measures of Constraint

Even if the hurdles under the law of countermeasures were cleared, confiscation collides with the customary international law of foreign sovereign immunity.

Under the 2004 UN Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and Their Property (reflecting customary international law on this point):

* **Heightened Status of Central Bank Reserves (Article 21(1)(c)):** Property of a central bank or other monetary authority of a foreign state held for central banking purposes is expressly categorized as property non-commercially utilized, enjoying heightened immunity from pre-judgment attachment and post-judgment execution.
* **Executive vs. Judicial Seizures:** Several domestic statutes (such as the US REPO Act) attempt to bypass sovereign immunity by authorizing direct executive seizure rather than requiring a judicial order. However, customary state immunity protects the sovereign entity and its sovereign property from state coercion generally; it does not disappear merely because an administrative cabinet member rather than a judge signs the forfeiture order.

Recognizing these risks, European authorities have largely avoided direct expropriation of the principal capital, opting instead for a windfall-profits model: taxing or skimming the accrued net interest and interest-rate spreads generated by immobilized funds at clearinghouses without formally revoking Russian sovereign ownership of the underlying debt securities.

---

## Systemic Consequences for the Global Reserve Architecture

The debate over repurposing sovereign assets underscores a widening rift between political objectives and established doctrines of public international law. 

If sovereign central bank reserves are treated as fair game for non-judicial confiscation by third-party states, the structural premise of international sovereign equality—and the legal predictability governing central bank cross-border reserve management—will experience lasting institutional fragmentation.
