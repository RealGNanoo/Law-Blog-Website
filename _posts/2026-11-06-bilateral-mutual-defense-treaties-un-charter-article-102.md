**PYONGYANG** — The entry into force of the Treaty on Comprehensive Strategic Partnership between the Russian Federation and the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea (DPRK) marks a structural resurgence of formal bilateral mutual assistance pacts. While multilateral collective security arrangements (such as NATO under Article 5 of the Washington Treaty) have dominated post-Cold War security discourse, the revival of bilateral mutual assistance instruments between nuclear-armed states introduces acute questions under public international law.

The central doctrinal controversy centers on how modern mutual defense guarantees reconcile with the prohibition on the threat or use of force under Article 2(4) of the United Nations Charter, the strict statutory preconditions for collective self-defense under Article 51, and the procedural effects of treaty registration under Article 102.

---

## 1. The Anatomy of Article 4: Collective Self-Defense vs. Automatic Belligerency

Article 4 of the Russia–DPRK Partnership Treaty provides:
> *"In case any one of the two sides is put in a state of war by an armed invasion from an individual state or several states, the other side shall provide military and other assistance with all means in its possession without delay in accordance with Article 51 of the UN Charter and the laws of the DPRK and the Russian Federation."*

The drafting reflects the text of Article 1 of the 1961 Soviet–North Korean Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Mutual Assistance, while deliberately inserting an explicit textual tether to **Article 51 of the UN Charter**.

From an international legal perspective, this reference serves an important legitimizing function:

* **The Armed Attack Prerequisite:** Under established customary international law (*Nicaragua v. United States*, ICJ 1986), the right of collective self-defense is not open-ended. It is contingent strictly on the occurrence of an **armed attack** against the requesting state. Internal instability, cross-border skirmishes falling below the threshold of gravity, or economic coercion cannot trigger lawful collective kinetic action.
* **The Declaration and Request Requirement:** Collective self-defense cannot be exercised unilaterally by an ally on a third state’s behalf. The ICJ affirmed in *Nicaragua* that the victim state must form and declare the assessment that it has been attacked, and must submit an explicit, formal request for military assistance. Article 4 attempts to operationalize this requirement by tying mutual aid to "a state of war by an armed invasion."
* **Necessity and Proportionality:** Even where an armed attack occurs, the military measures deployed by the assisting state remain bound by the customary principles of necessity and proportionality. Pledging assistance "with all means in its possession" cannot legally override the requirement that defensive measures must strictly be limited to repelling the specific attack.

## 2. Treaty Registration and Invocation Under UN Charter Article 102

The legal standing of bilateral security pacts within the United Nations institutional framework is governed by Chapter XVI of the UN Charter:

| UN Charter Provision | Procedural Mandate | Substantive Legal Consequence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Article 102(1)** | Mandatory submission of every international agreement entered into by a Member State to the UN Secretariat for registration and publication. | Ensures transparency and eliminates secret diplomacy in compliance with post-1945 international order. |
| **Article 102(2)** | Bar on invocation before UN organs. | Unregistered treaties cannot be invoked before the UN Security Council, General Assembly, or the International Court of Justice. |
| **Article 103** | Primacy of Charter obligations. | In the event of a conflict between a bilateral treaty and UN Charter commitments, Charter obligations prevail. |

Under Article 102(2), if a state fails to register a mutual assistance treaty with the Secretariat, the pact remains valid between the contracting parties (*pacta sunt servanda* under the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties), but cannot be invoked before any organ of the United Nations.

This restriction carries direct operational consequences: if an assisting state deploys military forces pursuant to a bilateral pact and subsequently faces condemnation before the Security Council or ICJ, it cannot cite an unregistered treaty as an affirmative legal defense. It must rely solely on independent customary rights of collective self-defense under Article 51.

## 3. Conflict of Obligations: Bilateral Treaties and Article 103 Primacy

The most significant legal challenge arises when bilateral mutual assistance pacts intersect with preexisting binding decisions of the United Nations Security Council.

Under Article 25 of the UN Charter, Member States agree to accept and carry out the decisions of the Security Council in accordance with the Charter. Furthermore, **Article 103** establishes absolute normative hierarchy:
> *"In the event of a conflict between the obligations of the Members of the United Nations under the present Charter and their obligations under any other international agreement, their obligations under the present Charter shall prevail."*

Over successive decades, the Security Council—operating under Chapter VII—adopted binding sanctions resolutions (including Resolutions 1718, 1874, 2270, and 2397) that expressly prohibit Member States from transferring conventional arms, dual-use military technology, and heavy weaponry to the DPRK, and mandate inspections of cargo entering or leaving North Korean ports:

* **The Normative Clash:** Where a bilateral treaty mandates the transfer of military equipment, joint technology development, or operational logistical aid, those commitments directly collide with Chapter VII arms embargoes.
* **The Legal Supremacy of Chapter VII:** Under Article 103, obligations flowing from Chapter VII resolutions supersede conflicting obligations in bilateral security treaties. A state cannot justify transferring prohibited military materiel or ballistic missile technology by invoking a bilateral mutual assistance agreement.

---

## Systemic Trajectory

The revival of bilateral mutual assistance instruments reflects a deepening fragmentation of the global security architecture. By embedding formal Article 51 self-defense language into bilateral instruments, states seek to construct an external veneer of Charter compliance. 

However, because Article 103 of the UN Charter subordinates all bilateral pacts to multilateral Chapter VII disciplines, attempts to build parallel collective defense axes operate in direct normative friction with the institutional collective security system designed in 1945.
