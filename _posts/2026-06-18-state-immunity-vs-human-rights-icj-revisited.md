**THE HAGUE** — Can a foreign state claim jurisdictional immunity in a domestic civil court when sued for serious violations of international humanitarian law, war crimes, or peremptory norms (*jus cogens*)?

Despite persistent efforts by national courts to establish an exception for atrocity crimes, the rule articulated by the International Court of Justice in *Jurisdictional Immunities of the State (Germany v. Italy: Greece intervening)* remains the governing doctrine of customary international law.

---

## 1. Substance vs. Procedure: The Jurisdictional Distinction

The primary theoretical argument against absolute sovereign immunity, advanced by the Italian Court of Cassation in *Ferrini*, asserted a normative hierarchy: because *jus cogens* norms occupy the highest tier of international law, subordinate procedural rules of state immunity must yield when such norms are breached.

The ICJ rejected that hierarchy on procedural grounds:

* **Procedural Character:** State immunity is a preliminary plea going strictly to jurisdiction. It determines whether a domestic court has the authority to adjudicate claims against a foreign sovereign; it does not assess the substantive legality of the underlying conduct.
* **Absence of Direct Conflict:** Because rules of immunity operate independently of substantive prohibitions against war crimes or torture, the Court found no direct normative conflict between the two bodies of law.
* **Sovereign Equality:** The doctrine is rooted in the maxim *par in parem imperium non habet* (equals have no jurisdiction over one another), codified under Article 2(1) of the UN Charter.

---

## 2. Enforcement Measures and Sovereign Assets

The strict procedural bar is under renewed pressure in two specific contexts:

1. **Central Bank Reserves:** Attempts by municipal authorities to immobilize and reassign foreign sovereign bank reserves to fund war reparations test the customary boundary separating jurisdictional immunity from immunity from execution.
2. **Domestic Statutory Exceptions:** National statutes that remove sovereign immunity for designated state sponsors of unlawful acts operate outside the multilateral consensus, raising unresolved jurisdictional questions under customary international law.
