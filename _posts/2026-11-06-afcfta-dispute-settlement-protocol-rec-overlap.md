**ACCRA** — As intra-African commercial flows expand under the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) and its Guided Trade Initiative, the operational readiness of its enforcement architecture has moved to the center of legal scrutiny. While the substantive protocols governing trade in goods, services, investment, intellectual property, and competition policy establish a single continental market, their legal effectiveness depends on the mechanism established to resolve inter-state controversies: the Protocol on Rules and Procedures on the Settlement of Disputes.

Modelled structurally on the World Trade Organization’s Dispute Settlement Understanding (DSU), the AfCFTA regime establishes an interstate dispute settlement mechanism featuring ad hoc panels and a standing seven-member Appellate Body. However, the integration of this continental mechanism into Africa’s preexisting regional architecture introduces complex questions of forum selection, overlapping adjudicative mandates, and the finality of arbitral awards.

---

## 1. Institutional Design and the Two-Tier Adjudicative Hierarchy

Under Article 20 of the AfCFTA Agreement and the Dispute Settlement Protocol, dispute settlement authority is vested in the Dispute Settlement Body (DSB), composed of representatives of all State Parties:

* **Ad Hoc Panels (Articles 9–19):** Where mandatory bilateral consultations fail to resolve a dispute within 60 days, the complaining State Party may request the establishment of a panel. Panels comprise three or five individuals selected from a indicative roster of qualified international trade law practitioners and scholars maintained by the AfCFTA Secretariat. Panels submit written reports containing factual determinations and legal findings within strict timeframes (typically six to nine months).
* **The Standing Appellate Body (Article 20):** In deliberate contrast to the paralyzed WTO Appellate Body, the AfCFTA Protocol preserves a functional two-tier adjudicative framework. The Appellate Body is composed of seven individuals appointed for a four-year term, renewable once. Three members serve on any given division to review issues of law covered in panel reports and legal interpretations developed by panels.
* **Negative Consensus Adoption:** Emulating DSU Articles 16.4 and 17.14, panel and Appellate Body reports are adopted by the DSB automatically unless the DSB decides by consensus not to adopt the report (reverse or negative consensus), ensuring that respondent states cannot unilaterally veto adverse adjudications.

## 2. Jurisdictional Interfaces with Regional Economic Communities (RECs)

The defining structural complexity of the AfCFTA dispute system is its relationship with established sub-regional judicial organs. Under the 1991 Abuja Treaty and Article 19(2) of the AfCFTA Agreement, existing Regional Economic Communities (RECs)—including ECOWAS, COMESA, the EAC, and SADC—serve as the building blocks of continental integration:

| Legal Parameter | Sub-Regional Courts (e.g., COMESA Court, EACJ) | AfCFTA Dispute Settlement Body |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Jurisdictional Basis** | Sub-regional treaties (e.g., EAC Treaty, COMESA Treaty). | Agreement Establishing the African Continental Free Trade Area and its Protocols. |
| **Standing Access** | Several REC courts permit direct private party and natural person access for treaty breaches. | Strictly state-to-state (Article 3(1) and Article 20(1)); private commercial entities have no direct *locus standi*. |
| **Appellate Tier** | Often operate as single-instance courts of record (or internal two-tier chambers without external review). | Standing continental Appellate Body reviewable under reverse consensus procedures. |
| **Norm Conflict Rule** | REC instruments govern matters within sub-regional customs unions and common markets. | In the event of inconsistency on AfCFTA matters, the AfCFTA Protocol prevails (Article 19(1)). |

To address potential conflicts over where a dispute must be litigated, Article 3(4) of the Dispute Settlement Protocol incorporates a modified *electa una via* (choice of forum) rule:
> *"A State Party which has invoked the rules and procedures of this Protocol with regards to a specific matter, shall not invoke another forum for dispute settlement on the same matter."*

While Article 3(4) prevents a complaining state from pursuing identical claims simultaneously before both a sub-regional court and the continental DSB once AfCFTA proceedings have commenced, it does not mandate exclusive AfCFTA jurisdiction at the outset. Because member states retain sovereign discretion to institute proceedings under applicable REC dispute mechanisms where parallel treaty obligations exist, forum-shopping remains a distinct legal possibility.

## 3. Absence of Direct Private Party Access and the Espousal Barrier

Unlike modern international investment arbitration frameworks or specialized regional economic tribunals where commercial entities can sue host states directly, the AfCFTA Dispute Settlement Protocol remains strictly intergovernmental. 

Private commercial entities, logistics operators, and cross-border traders who suffer financial injuries resulting from discriminatory border measures, unjustified non-tariff barriers (NTBs), or rules-of-origin misapplications have no standing before the DSB. 

To obtain legal redress under the Protocol, private actors must rely on the customary doctrine of diplomatic protection: persuading their national government to formally espouse their commercial grievance and initiate contentious state-to-state dispute proceedings. Because states weigh broader geopolitical, diplomatic, and trade considerations before hauling a neighboring sovereign before a panel, small and medium enterprises (SMEs) face an acute structural filter in accessing continental judicial relief.

---

## Doctrinal Implications for African Continental Trade Law

The operationalization of the AfCFTA Dispute Settlement Protocol represents an ambitious attempt to institutionalize a binding, rules-based trading order across 54 sovereign states. 

Its endurance will depend on how effectively its bodies resolve jurisdictional boundaries with regional economic community tribunals, and whether its non-tariff barrier reporting tools can resolve private commercial grievances before they require diplomatic espousal and formal state-to-state adjudication.
