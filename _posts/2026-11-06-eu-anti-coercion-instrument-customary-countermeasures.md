**BRUSSELS** — The operationalization of the European Union’s Anti-Coercion Instrument (ACI)—formally enacted under Regulation (EU) 2023/2675—marks a structural development in economic statecraft. Prompted by trade pressure campaigns, informal boycotts, and targeted secondary measures imposed against EU member states seeking to exercise sovereign policy choices, the regulation equips the European Commission with authority to deploy commercial countermeasures against third states.

While framed as a defensive mechanism of last resort, the instrument raises fundamental questions under public international law: What constitutes unlawful economic coercion under customary international law? And to what extent does the law of state responsibility authorize unilateral countermeasures that bypass multilateral dispute settlement at the World Trade Organization?

---

## 1. Defining Economic Coercion: The Customary Non-Intervention Principle

The primary legal hurdle in operationalizing the ACI is establishing that foreign economic pressure constitutes an internationally wrongful act. 

Under Article 2(1) of the Regulation, economic coercion exists where a third country applies or threatens to apply measures affecting trade or investment to interfere in the legitimate sovereign choices of the Union or a member state, with the objective of obtaining the cessation, modification, or adoption of a particular act.

In public international law, the prohibition of intervention is a corollary of sovereign equality, codified in UN General Assembly Resolution 2625 (XXV) (The Friendly Relations Declaration) and affirmed in the ICJ’s *Nicaragua* judgment:

* **The Nicaragua Test:** Coercion is the defining element of prohibited intervention. An intervention is wrongful when it uses methods of coercion in regard to choices that each state is entitled, by the principle of state sovereignty, to decide freely (such as foreign relations, domestic legislation, or diplomatic representation).
* **The Threshold Dilemma:** Customary international law does not prohibit tough trade negotiations, tariff renegotiations, or the lawful withdrawal of economic benefits. To transform economic statecraft into an unlawful intervention, the coercion must reach a coercive severity that effectively deprives the sovereign state of its free will. Because states rarely acknowledge political pressure campaigns on paper—relying instead on informal customs delays, phytosanitary holds, or disguised administrative hurdles—proving the required coercive intent under customary evidentiary standards remains an acute challenge.

## 2. Countermeasures Under ARSIWA: Inducement vs. Retaliation

Where the EU formally identifies an act of foreign economic coercion, the ACI authorizes Union response measures—including tariff surcharges, import/export restrictions, exclusions from public procurement, and restrictions on foreign direct investment.

Under general international law, the legality of these otherwise treaty-breaching trade restrictions depends on their characterization as **countermeasures** under the International Law Commission’s Articles on State Responsibility (ARSIWA):

| ARSIWA Parameter | Statutory Rule (Articles 49–53) | Operational Design in the ACI |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Objective / Purpose** | Strictly limited to inducing the responsible state to comply with its international obligations (Article 49(1)). | Conceived primarily as deterrence and leverage; response measures are triggered only after mandatory engagement and dialogue fail. |
| **Reversibility** | Must permit the resumption of performance of obligations once the breach ceases (Article 49(3)). | Measures must be suspended or terminated immediately if the third country ceases coercion or provides adequate reparation. |
| **Proportionality** | Must be commensurate with the injury suffered, taking into account the gravity of the wrongful act and rights in question (Article 51). | Article 9 mandates that EU response measures must not exceed what is proportionate to the injury caused by the third country's measure. |

A key doctrinal innovation of the ACI is that it treats economic countermeasures not as punitive retaliation, but as an institutionalized inducement mechanism. By requiring formal prior notification, mandatory consultation periods, and built-in sunset provisions, the Regulation seeks to satisfy the procedural preconditions of ARSIWA Article 52.

## 3. The Multilateral Interface: Countermeasures vs. DSU Article 23

The most significant systemic tension surrounding the ACI is its relationship with World Trade Organization disciplines. 

If the EU responds to third-country economic coercion by raising tariffs above bound rates or imposing selective import restrictions, those measures *prima facie* breach GATT Article I (MFN), Article II (Schedules of Concessions), or Article XI (Elimination of Quantitative Restrictions):

* **The Exclusivity of DSU Article 23:** Article 23.1 of the WTO Dispute Settlement Understanding establishes that when members seek the redress of a violation of obligations under the covered agreements, they must have recourse to, and abide by, the rules and procedures of the DSU. Unilateral determinations of violation and unilateral suspensions of concessions are expressly prohibited.
* **The "General International Law" Defense:** The European Commission maintains that where third-country coercion breaches customary international law (the non-intervention principle) rather than a specific WTO covered agreement, the Union retains the sovereign right under customary state responsibility to take countermeasures outside the WTO framework.
* **The Lex Specialis Debate:** Critics contend that if the response measure itself takes the form of trade restrictions on goods or services, it falls within the scope of the WTO agreements, making the DSU a *lex specialis* that displaces general customary countermeasures. If a targeted third state challenges an ACI measure before a WTO panel, the EU would be forced to defend the action under the narrow security exceptions of GATT Article XXI, as panels cannot readily enforce non-WTO customary countermeasures to excuse direct GATT violations.

## Doctrinal Implications for International Economic Law

The European Anti-Coercion Instrument reflects a broader fragmentation in global economic governance. 

By constructing a domestic statutory architecture that operationalizes ARSIWA countermeasures to defend sovereign decision-making against economic pressure, the EU is testing the boundary between traditional multilateralism and geoeconomic self-help. The endurance of this legal framework will ultimately depend on whether international adjudicative bodies recognize general customary countermeasures as a valid defense against claims of treaty non-compliance in cross-border trade.
