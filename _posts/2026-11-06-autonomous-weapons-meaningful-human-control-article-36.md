**GENEVA** — The integration of artificial intelligence and automated target recognition into uninhabited combat platforms has shifted the debate within the Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on Emerging Technologies in the Area of Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS). As loitering munitions, automated air-defense networks, and algorithmic target prioritization tools see widespread deployment across contemporary armed conflicts, discussions under the framework of the 1980 Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) have moved beyond abstract definitions to concrete regulatory parameters.

The core legal controversy centers on the principle of **meaningful human control** (or "appropriate levels of human judgment"): Can fully autonomous weapons systems—capable of selecting and engaging targets without human intervention—comply with the cardinal rules of distinction, proportionality, and precautions under International Humanitarian Law (IHL)? And how does Article 36 of Additional Protocol I (API) constrain the development and deployment of algorithmic weapon systems?

---

## 1. Compliance with the Cardinal Principles of IHL

Under customary international humanitarian law and Additional Protocol I, any attack during armed conflict must comply with three interrelated legal benchmarks:

* **The Principle of Distinction (API Articles 48, 51(2), and 52(2)):** Combatants must distinguish at all times between the civilian population and combatants, and between civilian objects and military objectives. In complex urban environments, distinguishing between a combatant, a civilian directly participating in hostilities, and a non-combatant carrying personal protective gear requires contextual reasoning that algorithmic sensor suites—relying on machine-learning pattern recognition and visual proxies—struggle to resolve reliably.
* **The Principle of Proportionality (API Articles 51(5)(b) and 57(2)(a)(iii)):** Prohibits attacks expected to cause incidental loss of civilian life, injury to civilians, or damage to civilian objects that would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. Proportionality requires a qualitative value judgment rather than a purely mathematical computation: weighing military advantage against civilian harm is fundamentally a contextual legal assessment assigned to a human commander.
* **The Obligation to Take Precautions in Attack (API Article 57):** Those who plan or decide upon an attack must take all feasible precautions in the choice of means and methods of warfare to avoid, and in any event to minimize, incidental civilian harm. Crucially, Article 57(2)(b) mandates that an attack must be suspended or cancelled if it becomes apparent that the objective is not a military one or that the attack violates proportionality. An autonomous weapon severed from human communication links cannot independently evaluate dynamic alterations in civilian density once deployed.

## 2. Defining "Meaningful Human Control"

To operationalize IHL requirements in algorithmic warfare, legal scholars and the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) formulated the concept of **meaningful human control (MHC)**. The doctrine divides the human-machine relationship into three structural components:

| Dimension of Control | Operational Requirement | Failure / Breach Scenario |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Temporal and Geographic Limits** | The operating parameters (time of operation, geographic perimeter) must be strictly delimited by a human operator prior to activation. | Deploying an autonomous system into an open-ended area for an indefinite duration, allowing algorithms to determine target selection autonomously over time. |
| **Environmental Predictability** | The operational environment must be sufficiently constrained (e.g., open sea, high airspace) to minimize the risk of civilian presence. | Deploying autonomous terminal-engagement munitions in densely populated urban centers with high civilian co-location. |
| **Abort / Intervention Capacity** | The human commander must retain real-time situational awareness and the physical capability to abort or recall the attack if conditions change. | Severing human-in-the-loop links, rendering the weapon an unrecallable "fire-and-forget" lethal platform. |

Under this framework, autonomous systems that target specific military *objects* (such as radar installations or armored vehicles) in environments devoid of civilians can satisfy IHL if subjected to narrow geographic and temporal constraints. 

Conversely, autonomous weapons designed to target *persons* based on biometric data, thermal imaging, or algorithmic profiling are increasingly viewed by international bodies as fundamentally incompatible with human dignity and the Martens Clause, because reducing human life to algorithmic data points deprives individuals of legal subjecthood.

## 3. Weapon Reviews Under Article 36 of Additional Protocol I

The primary preventative legal check in the law of armed conflict is codified in Article 36 of Additional Protocol I:
> *"In the study, development, acquisition or adoption of a new weapon, means or method of warfare, a High Contracting Party is under an obligation to determine whether its employment would, in some or all circumstances, be prohibited by this Protocol or by any other rule of international law applicable to the High Contracting Party."*

The application of Article 36 to autonomous weapons presents unique technical and legal hurdles:

1. **The "Black Box" Problem and Explainability:** Deep neural networks and adaptive machine-learning models frequently produce unpredictable outputs based on uninterpretable statistical correlations. If state legal advisers cannot predict how an algorithm will behave across edge-case operational scenarios, the state cannot lawfully certify that the weapon will comply with IHL under all intended circumstances.
2. **Machine Learning and Post-Deployment Drift:** Traditional weapons remain static after procurement. In contrast, artificial intelligence systems that learn or update their parameters iteratively post-deployment alter their targeting logic over time. Such modifications constitute the "adoption of a new weapon" under Article 36, requiring continuous legal re-evaluation throughout the weapon’s lifecycle.
3. **The State Responsibility Gap:** Under ARSIWA Article 4, conduct of state organs (including armed forces) is attributable to the state. However, if an autonomous system commits an unlawful strike due to a software error, establishing individual criminal liability before the International Criminal Court under Article 28 of the Rome Statute (Command Responsibility) requires proving that the commander knew or should have known of the imminent commission of the war crime. If the commander lacked meaningful control over the system's targeting calculation, criminal accountability risks dissipation into institutional negligence.

## Broader Normative Outlook

The debate over Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems illustrates the structural tension between tactical military efficiency and the humanitarian constraints of public international law. 

While negotiations at the CCW in Geneva continue to face diplomatic gridlock between major military powers favoring non-binding codes of conduct and coalitions demanding an outright ban treaty, customary international law does not exist in an normative vacuum. By enforcing the strict requirements of meaningful human control through comprehensive Article 36 legal reviews, states remain legally obligated to ensure that life-and-death targeting decisions remain an exclusively human prerogative.
