**VIENNA** — The ten-year milestone of United Nations Security Council Resolution 2231 (2015)—which endorsed the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA)—marked the formal "Termination Day" specified in the resolution’s text. Under the original 2015 roadmap, October 18, 2025, was designed to conclude the Security Council’s formal consideration of the Iranian nuclear issue and permanently terminate the dormant UN sanctions provisions contained in prior resolutions dating back to 2006.

However, the invocation of the contentious "snapback" mechanism by European participants (the E3: France, Germany, and the United Kingdom) prior to the sunset date, countered by procedural challenges from Tehran, Moscow, and Beijing, has fractured the multilateral sanctions regime. 

With the legal status of Chapter VII mandates disputed in New York, the operational friction has migrated to international waters, where unilateral maritime interdictions of energy shipments and alleged proliferation materiel in the Persian Gulf and Gulf of Oman directly test the law of the sea.

---

## 1. The Anatomy of Resolution 2231: Paragraph 11 Snapback vs. Termination Day

Paragraph 11 and 12 of UNSCR 2231 established an unconventional procedural mechanism:

* **The Reverse Veto Architecture:** If a JCPOA participant state notifies the Security Council of an issue deemed to constitute "significant non-performance" of commitments, the Security Council must vote on a resolution to *continue* the sanctions lifting within 30 days. If no such resolution is adopted—which can be ensured by the permanent veto of any one P5 member—the provisions of prior resolutions (1696, 1737, 1747, 1803, 1929, and 2224) are automatically reimposed.
* **The "Participant State" Controversy:** When the United States attempted to unilaterally trigger snapback in 2020 after having ceased participation in the JCPOA in 2018, the Security Council presidency and an overwhelming majority of members rejected the notification, deeming it legally ineffective because the US had renounced its status as a participant under the agreement.
* **The E3 Invocation and Termination Friction:** When European signatories subsequently advanced non-performance notifications citing uranium enrichment levels and IAEA verification impasses, the legal landscape diverged. While the E3 remained indisputable JCPOA participants, opposing council members argued that European non-compliance with domestic sanctions-lifting commitments and the wider political context vitiated the good-faith invocation of Paragraph 11.

Consequently, the international community faces two irreconcilable legal interpretations: Western jurisdictions asserting that binding Chapter VII asset freezes, travel bans, and missile technology restrictions have been legally revived, while Iran and non-aligned states contend that the resolution’s ten-year clock expired on October 18, 2025, terminating the Council's oversight.

## 2. Maritime Interdictions and the Law of the Sea

In the absence of universally recognized Chapter VII authorizations (such as those previously provided under Resolution 1929, which authorized high-seas inspections of vessels suspected of carrying prohibited cargo), enforcement of unilateral or contested sanctions falls back on the general regime of the law of the sea:

| Maritime Zone / Doctrine | Governing UNCLOS Regime | Legal Threshold for Foreign Warship Interdiction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **High Seas / EEZ (Right of Visit)** | UNCLOS Article 110 | Strictly limited to suspected piracy, slave trading, unauthorized broadcasting, vessels without nationality, or flying a fraudulent flag. Suspected sanctions evasion does not confer a right of boarding. |
| **Transit Passage (Strait of Hormuz)** | UNCLOS Articles 37–44 | Coastal states cannot impede, hamper, or suspend transit passage for vessels engaged in continuous and expeditious transit, even during diplomatic disputes. |
| **Flag State Exclusive Jurisdiction** | UNCLOS Article 92 | Vessels on the high seas are subject to the exclusive jurisdiction of their flag state unless an express treaty or bilateral boarding agreement exists. |

Under customary international law, unilateral domestic sanctions legislation (such as US secondary sanctions administered by OFAC) has no extraterritorial enforcement authority against foreign-flagged commercial vessels operating outside the territorial waters of the regulating state. 

When naval forces intercept, divert, or board commercial tankers on the high seas without the flag state’s express consent or a clear UNCLOS Article 110 ground, the interception constitutes an internationally wrongful act under the law of the sea, generating state responsibility for unlawful detention.

## 3. Asymmetric Countermeasures and "Shadow Fleet" Jurisdictions

To circumvent enforcement operations, commercial operators transporting Iranian petroleum have relied extensively on flag-hopping, fraudulent corporate registrations, and automated identification system (AIS) manipulation within open registries.

This has triggered secondary legal friction:

1. **Vessels Without Nationality (Article 110(1)(d)):** When a vessel uses false documentation, registers under a flag without valid state authorization, or concurrently flies more than one flag according to convenience (Article 92(2)), it loses the protection of flag-state exclusivity. Warships of any nation hold the customary right to visit and inspect stateless vessels on the high seas.
2. **Coastal State Self-Defense Claims (Article 51):** Where maritime boarding occurs within regional waters, states frequently invoke national self-defense or preventive security exceptions. However, economic sanctions enforcement cannot be subsumed under Article 51 of the UN Charter, which requires an actual or imminent armed attack to justify coercive kinetic boardings.

## Broader Doctrinal Trajectory

The fragmentation of the UNSCR 2231 framework illustrates the fragility of multilateral non-proliferation mechanisms when structural consensus breaks down among the permanent members of the Security Council. 

Without universally recognized Chapter VII boarding authorities, efforts to enforce non-proliferation restrictions at sea operate in direct tension with the cornerstone principle of freedom of navigation under UNCLOS. The resulting practice of unilateral interdictions and tit-for-tat vessel detentions highlights how geopolitical disputes transform narrow technical provisions of treaty law into open maritime confrontations.
