**GENEVA** — With the European Union’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) transitioning from its initial reporting phase into full financial levy collection, unilateral environmental trade measures have entered the core of multilateral trade scrutiny. Designed to equalize the price of carbon paid by domestic EU producers under the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and foreign importers of carbon-intensive goods—specifically cement, steel, aluminum, fertilizers, hydrogen, and electricity—the regulation aims to prevent "carbon leakage."

However, major developing exporters and trading partners have questioned the measure's compatibility with multilateral trade rules, arguing that the structure of the financial charge operates as an extraterritorial tariff barrier that conflicts with fundamental non-discrimination obligations under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT 1994).

---

## 1. Classification Under GATT: Article II vs. Article III:2

The initial legal threshold for evaluating CBAM is whether the requirement to purchase surrendered certificates constitutes an internal tax adjustment or an ordinary customs duty:

* **Border Tax Adjustment Under Article III:2 and Article II:2(a):** Under GATT Article II:2(a), members may impose on imported products a charge equivalent to an internal tax imposed on like domestic products in respect of an article from which the imported product has been manufactured. If CBAM is classified as a legitimate border tax adjustment mirroring the internal costs of the EU ETS, it must satisfy the strict standard of **National Treatment** under Article III:2, first sentence (no excess taxation on like products).
* **Ordinary Customs Duty (Article II:1(b)):** If adjudicators find that CBAM is not truly an internal tax applied equally at the border—because foreign producers cannot access identical free allocation allowances or because the surrender mechanism operates under distinct regulatory machinery—the levy would be categorized as a customs duty. In that scenario, any charge exceeding the EU’s bound tariff rates in its WTO Schedule of Concessions breaches Article II:1(b).

---

## 2. Like Products and the Process and Production Methods (PPM) Dilemma

Under established WTO jurisprudence (*EC – Asbestos*, *US – Gasoline*), products that share identical physical characteristics, tariff classifications, and end-uses are traditionally treated as "like products" under GATT Article I (Most-Favored-Nation) and Article III (National Treatment).

CBAM differentiates between imported goods based on **embedded carbon emissions**—an unincorporated Process and Production Method (PPM). A metric ton of steel produced via an electric arc furnace and a metric ton produced via a traditional coal blast furnace are chemically and functionally identical upon arrival at an EU port:

* **The Non-Discrimination Hurdle:** If carbon intensity cannot legally differentiate "like" steel products, imposing certificate surrender obligations on high-emission foreign steel while low-emission domestic or preferred foreign steel enters unburdened constitutes *prima facie* de facto discrimination under Article III:4 or Article I:1.
* **Country Exemptions and MFN:** CBAM exempts third countries that participate in the EU ETS or maintain fully linked domestic carbon markets (such as Switzerland). Importers from non-exempt nations argue this differential treatment violates Article I:1 by conferring a commercial advantage based on national origin rather than product characteristics.

---

## 3. Justification Under GATT Article XX General Exceptions

Because CBAM faces serious hurdles under Article I and Article III, the legal defense rests almost entirely on the general exceptions of GATT Article XX, specifically:

* **Article XX(g) (Conservation of Exhaustible Natural Resources):** The measure must relate to the conservation of a natural resource and be made effective in conjunction with restrictions on domestic production or consumption. The atmosphere and climate stability are established as exhaustible natural resources (*US – Gasoline*, *US – Shrimp*). Because the EU simultaneous limits domestic emissions via the ETS cap, satisfying the "relating to" nexus is doctrinally feasible.
* **Article XX(b) (Protection of Human, Animal, or Plant Life or Health):** The EU must demonstrate that CBAM is "necessary" to achieve its climate objectives, requiring a genuine balance between the measure's contribution to emissions reduction and its restrictive impact on international commerce.

---

## 4. The Chapeau of Article XX: Arbitrary and Unjustifiable Discrimination

Even if CBAM falls within paragraphs (b) or (g), it must satisfy the introductory clause (the Chapeau) of Article XX, which prohibits measures applied in a manner that constitutes:
1. Arbitrary discrimination between countries where the same conditions prevail;
2. Unjustifiable discrimination between countries; or
3. A disguised restriction on international trade.

| Chapeau Element | EU Regulatory Argument | Developing Country Objection |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Accounting for Foreign Policies** | CBAM permits deductions if an importer can demonstrate that an explicit carbon price was already paid in the country of origin. | The deduction applies only to explicit carbon taxes, ignoring non-price regulatory instruments, renewable subsidies, or developing country baselines. |
| **Common But Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR)** | Environmental protection requires an objective, universal carbon price across all global supply chains to prevent circumvention. | By imposing uniform compliance costs regardless of development status, CBAM undermines the CBDR-RC principle codified in Article 3.5 of the UNFCCC. |
| **Use of Generated Revenue** | Funds support the EU budget and administrative operation of the carbon reporting registry. | Revenue is not redirected toward climate mitigation or technical capacity-building in affected exporting developing nations. |

Under landmark jurisprudence in *US – Shrimp*, a trade measure fails the Chapeau if it requires exporting states to adopt essentially the same regulatory program without considering whether different local conditions achieve equivalent conservation results. If the EU rejects foreign carbon mitigation measures that rely on statutory standards rather than explicit emissions taxes, panels could find the application of CBAM to constitute unjustifiable discrimination under the Chapeau.

---

## Implications for International Economic Policy

The dispute over CBAM represents a structural conflict between multilateral trade commitments and unilateral climate action. If the World Trade Organization’s adjudicative organs—whether formal panels or MPIA arbitral tribunals—condemn border carbon adjustments, states will face acute pressure to renegotiate GATT exceptions to accommodate climate legislation. 

Conversely, if CBAM is upheld under Article XX, it will establish a binding precedent permitting states to use border tax mechanisms to project domestic environmental standards across transnational supply chains.
