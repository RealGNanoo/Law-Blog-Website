**THE HAGUE** — The application of international criminal law to siege tactics, naval blockades, and restrictions on humanitarian access has brought the war crime of starvation to the forefront of proceedings before the International Criminal Court (ICC). While historically treated as an incidental byproduct of kinetic hostilities or military encirclement, contemporary prosecutorial practice has focused directly on the statutory elements of starvation as an independent method of warfare.

The central legal inquiries divide into three interrelated questions: What constitutes the material element (*actus reus*) of depriving civilians of objects indispensable to their survival? What standard of intent (*mens rea*) is required to prove that starvation was deployed as a deliberate method of warfare? And how does the customary prohibition operate across international and non-international armed conflicts?

---

## 1. The Statutory Architecture: Additional Protocols and the Rome Statute

The prohibition of starvation originated as a treaty rule in the 1977 Additional Protocols to the Geneva Conventions:

* **Additional Protocol I (Article 54) and Additional Protocol II (Article 14):** Both instruments codify the foundational rule: *"Starvation of civilians as a method of warfare is prohibited."* Paragraph 2 of Article 54 details the operational scope, forbidding parties to *"attack, destroy, remove or render useless objects indispensable to the survival of the civilian population, such as foodstuffs, agricultural areas for the production of foodstuffs, crops, livestock, drinking water installations and supplies and irrigation works."*
* **The Rome Statute Codification (Article 8(2)(b)(xxv)):** Starvation was elevated to an explicit war crime in international armed conflicts (IACs), defined as:
  > *"Intentionally using starvation of civilians as a method of warfare by depriving them of objects indispensable to their survival, including wilfully impeding relief supplies as provided for under the Geneva Conventions."*

Crucially, the crime does not require that victims actually perish from malnutrition. The offense is completed once the perpetrator deprives the civilian population of indispensable objects or willfully impedes humanitarian relief with the requisite intent to use deprivation as a weapon.

---

## 2. The Material Elements: Indispensable Objects and Willful Impediment

The Elements of Crimes for Article 8(2)(b)(xxv) establish two distinct conduct components:

| Conduct Element | Legal Benchmark | Operational Application |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Deprivation of Indispensable Objects** | Must target items necessary to sustain human physical life (food, clean water, medical supplies, fuel for purification). | Direct destruction of agricultural infrastructure, systematic decommissioning of water treatment facilities, or disabling of civil bakeries. |
| **Willful Impediment of Relief Supplies** | Arbitrary refusal of consent or deliberate administrative blockage of impartial humanitarian operations. | Imposing bureaucratic bottlenecks, closing border crossings without valid military necessity, or attacking designated humanitarian convoys. |

Under international humanitarian law (specifically Fourth Geneva Convention Article 23 and API Article 70), humanitarian relief operations must be undertaken when the civilian population is experiencing inadequate supply. 

While a belligerent retains a legitimate right of control—including the authority to verify that relief shipments do not contain contraband or military supplies—withholding consent to relief operations is unlawful if done arbitrarily. Where a party conditions the entry of essential food and medical assistance on political concessions or uses administrative delays to induce hunger, the impediment ceases to be a valid exercise of regulatory verification and becomes an intentional act of deprivation.

## 3. The Mens Rea Threshold: Purpose vs. Knowledge Under Article 30

The primary evidentiary obstacle in prosecuting starvation is proving the mental element. Under Article 30 of the Rome Statute, a person has criminal intent if they mean to engage in the conduct and mean to cause the resulting consequence, or are aware that the consequence will occur in the ordinary course of events.

In starvation prosecutions, this generates two competing doctrinal interpretations:

* **The Specific Intent Standard (Purpose):** Defense counsel argue that the phrase *"as a method of warfare"* requires proof that the perpetrator acted with the specific, affirmative purpose of using hunger as a weapon to weaken the adversary, coerce civilian capitulation, or achieve a military objective. Under this strict reading, if food deprivation is merely the foreseeable incidental consequence of an otherwise lawful military siege aimed solely at combatants, the *mens rea* for starvation is absent.
* **The Dolus Directus Standard (Knowledge and Foreseeability):** Conversely, modern prosecutorial strategy maintains that under the Elements of Crimes, it is sufficient that the perpetrator engaged in the deprivation intentionally, fully aware that civilian starvation would ensue in the ordinary course of events. When military authorities systematically interdict food supply corridors into densely populated enclaves over extended durations, knowledge that starvation will inevitably occur satisfies the *mens rea* threshold—motive and secondary military aims cannot excuse deliberate deprivation.

Furthermore, military necessity cannot serve as an affirmative defense. While Article 54(5) of API permits a state to destroy indispensable objects within its *own* sovereign territory under extreme military necessity to defend against foreign invasion ("scorched earth" policies), no military necessity exception exists to justify depriving civilian populations in occupied or enemy territory.

---

## 4. The NIAC Harmonization: The 2019 Rome Statute Amendment

Historically, Article 8(2)(b)(xxv) applied exclusively to international armed conflicts, creating a jurisdictional vacuum given that most modern blockades and siege scenarios occur in non-international armed conflicts (NIACs).

To resolve this asymmetry:
* **The Switzerland Amendment (2019):** The Assembly of States Parties adopted an amendment inserting Article 8(2)(e)(xix), extending the jurisdiction of the ICC over the war crime of starvation to conflicts not of an international character.
* **Customary Status:** Even for states that have not yet formally ratified the 2019 amendment, the prohibition of starvation in NIACs is established under customary international law (reflected in ICRC Customary IHL Study, Rule 53). Consequently, individual criminal responsibility for starvation in internal armed conflicts forms part of the customary acquis enforced by international tribunals.

---

## Broader Jurisprudential Trajectory

The doctrinal crystallization of starvation as an independent war crime marks a fundamental check on siege warfare and asymmetric blockades. 

By confirming that the crime is triggered by intentional deprivation and the arbitrary obstruction of humanitarian relief—regardless of whether mortality rates reach famine definitions—the International Criminal Court has reinforced the principle that civilian survival needs cannot be subordinated to operational military pressure.
