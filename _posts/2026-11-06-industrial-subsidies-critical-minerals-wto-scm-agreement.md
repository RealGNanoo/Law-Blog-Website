**GENEVA** — The global race to secure critical mineral supply chains—encompassing lithium, cobalt, nickel, and rare earth elements—has prompted major economies to deploy state-directed industrial subsidies. Legislative instruments such as the United States Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), the European Union’s Critical Raw Materials Act, and parallel subsidy frameworks across East Asia reflect a strategic pivot: pairing climate decarbonization targets with reshoring industrial manufacturing capacity.

By conditioning fiscal benefits, production tax credits, and consumer rebates on domestic assembly and preferential mineral sourcing, these statutory regimes intersect directly with multilateral disciplines under the World Trade Organization’s Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures (SCM Agreement) and GATT Article III.

---

## 1. Classification Under the SCM Agreement: The Local Content Prohibition

The fundamental discipline governing trade-distorting state support is codified in Article 3.1(b) of the SCM Agreement, which prohibits:
> *"Subsidies contingent, whether solely or as one of several other conditions, upon the use of domestic over imported goods."*

Unlike actionable subsidies under Part III of the SCM Agreement—which require an applicant to prove specific adverse trade effects (serious prejudice, nullification, or impairment of benefits)—prohibited subsidies under Article 3 are treated as *per se* inconsistent with WTO law once specificity and contingency are demonstrated:

* **De Jure Contingency:** When a statute explicitly conditions a tax credit or cash transfer on a requirement that a percentage of critical mineral value be extracted, processed, or recycled domestically (or within designated free-trade partners), the measure satisfies *de jure* local content contingency.
* **The Non-Severability of Dual Criteria:** Several modern subsidy statutes split tax credits into two tranches: one portion tied to battery component assembly, and another tied to critical mineral sourcing. Even if the mineral requirement allows inputs from select bilateral partners, excluding other WTO members solely on geographic origin conflicts with Article 3.1(b) and GATT Article III:4.

## 2. Bilateral Critical Minerals Agreements and GATT Article XXIV

To mitigate friction with allied economies excluded from domestic subsidy perimeters, states have negotiated targeted "Critical Minerals Agreements" (CMAs)—plurilateral executive pacts establishing shared labor and environmental baselines for mineral extraction without broad tariff elimination.

This creates a serious procedural dilemma under WTO exception architecture:

| Statutory Parameter | Full Regional Free Trade Agreement (GATT Article XXIV) | Targeted Critical Minerals Agreement (CMA) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Trade Coverage** | Must eliminate duties and restrictions on "substantially all the trade" between parties (Article XXIV:8(b)). | Restricted strictly to designated raw minerals and supply chain processing standards. |
| **Tariff Concessions** | Enforces mutual, comprehensive tariff reductions across industrial and agricultural chapters. | Maintains external tariff schedules; merely serves as a statutory qualification gateway for domestic subsidies. |
| **MFN Exemption Validity** | Provides an affirmative defense against Most-Favored-Nation (MFN) claims under GATT Article I:1. | Cannot invoke Article XXIV; vulnerable to non-discrimination challenges by excluded mineral exporters. |

Exporters not party to these bilateral arrangements argue that extending favorable subsidy eligibility to designated mineral partners while denying it to others constitutes discriminatory treatment under GATT Article I:1 that cannot be shielded by Article XXIV.

## 3. Absence of Environmental Exceptions in the SCM Agreement

A defining structural limitation of the WTO subsidy framework is the absence of a general environmental defense. While GATT 1994 contains Article XX (permitting trade-restrictive measures necessary to protect human, animal, or plant life, or relating to the conservation of exhaustible natural resources), the SCM Agreement is a distinct specialized agreement:

* **Non-Application of GATT Article XX to the SCM Agreement:** In *China – Rare Earths* and *India – Solar Cells*, dispute panels and the Appellate Body affirmed that GATT Article XX exceptions cannot be imported wholesale into the SCM Agreement unless the specific text of the covered agreement explicitly incorporates them.
* **The Lapse of Article 8 (Non-Actionable Subsidies):** When the WTO was established, Article 8.2(c) of the SCM Agreement created a temporary "green light" category for environmental subsidies—exempting state assistance promoting adaptation of existing facilities to new environmental requirements. However, under Article 31 of the SCM Agreement, this provision expired in 1999 without consensus to renew it.

Consequently, respondents facing SCM Article 3 claims cannot mount an affirmative defense grounded in climate necessity or carbon abatement. A panel evaluating a local-content-linked tax incentive must restrict its review to whether the financial contribution confers a benefit and is contingent upon domestic goods.

## Systemic Pressures on Multilateral Subsidy Disciplines

The disconnect between 1994 multilateral subsidy rules and contemporary decarbonization imperatives has created systemic enforcement gridlock. With the formal Appellate Body suspended, respondent states frequently invoke MPIA arbitration or permit dispute findings to remain unadopted, proceeding unilaterally with domestic industrial plans.

The endurance of the multilateral trading system will increasingly depend on whether WTO members can negotiate a modernized subsidy framework—one that restores legal certainty for renewable infrastructure investments while establishing clear disciplines against discriminatory sourcing requirements.
