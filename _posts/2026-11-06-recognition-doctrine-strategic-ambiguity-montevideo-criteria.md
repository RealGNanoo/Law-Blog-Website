**TAIPEI** — In international legal doctrine, few concepts produce as much operational friction as the gap between the objective criteria of statehood and the political act of diplomatic recognition. While sovereign states routinely navigate informal ties with contested political entities, the legal status of self-governing territories that possess permanent populations, defined territories, and functioning constitutional administrations—yet lack universal diplomatic recognition or a seat at the United Nations—remains governed by the intersection of customary recognition doctrine and domestic statutory law.

The central doctrinal question divides into two inquiries: does statehood exist independently of foreign recognition under customary international law, and how do domestic legislative frameworks construct binding commercial, security, and diplomatic relations with an entity whose formal sovereignty remains unacknowledged?

---

## 1. Declaratory vs. Constitutive Theories: The Montevideo Criteria

The fundamental debate in the law of statehood turns on the competing theories of recognition:

* **The Declaratory Theory (Article 3, Montevideo Convention):** Statehood is an objective question of fact. An entity that satisfies the criteria codified in Article 1 of the 1933 Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States—(a) a permanent population, (b) a defined territory, (c) an effective government, and (d) the capacity to enter into relations with other states—is a state under international law. Recognition by third states is merely declaratory, serving as a political acknowledgment of a preexisting legal fact.
* **The Constitutive Theory:** Statehood is not achieved through internal effectiveness alone; legal personality is conferred only through the affirmative consent and formal recognition of existing sovereign states. Without reciprocal diplomatic recognition, an entity remains an anomalous territorial entity lacking sovereign rights under the international legal order.

Customary international law largely favors the declaratory approach (*Badinter Arbitration Commission*, Opinion No. 1): the existence or disappearance of a state is a question of fact, and the effects of recognition by other states are purely declaratory. 

However, in contemporary state practice, an entity possessing complete internal effectiveness may find its external capacity (*capacity to enter into relations*) structurally constrained if third states decline formal bilateral engagement due to political sensitivities or obligations owed to competing claimants under bilateral communiqués.

## 2. Domestic Statutory Surrogates: The Taiwan Relations Act Architecture

To manage trade, security, and diplomatic interaction without triggering formal recognition, states have constructed specialized domestic legislative instruments. The benchmark remains the United States Taiwan Relations Act of 1979 (TRA, 22 U.S.C. §§ 3301–3316):

| Legal Dimension | Traditional Sovereign Recognition | Domestic Statutory Framework (TRA Model) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Legal Status of Municipal Laws** | Domestic statutes of foreign sovereigns are applied under international comity and foreign law recognition rules. | Section 4(a) mandates that absence of diplomatic relations shall not affect the application of US laws to Taiwan. |
| **Capacity to Sue and Be Sued** | Governed by customary foreign sovereign immunities and diplomatic standing before municipal courts. | Section 4(b)(7) explicitly confers standing before domestic courts, regardless of executive recognition status. |
| **Treaty and Property Succession** | Bilateral treaties survive or terminate based on state succession rules and diplomatic recognition. | Section 4(c) preserves the validity of pre-1979 bilateral treaties and international agreements in force. |
| **Diplomatic Representation** | Embassies and accredited ambassadors operating under the 1961 Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations. | Non-governmental corporate instrumentalities (e.g., the American Institute in Taiwan) staffed by personnel on leave. |

Under Section 4(b)(1) of the TRA, whenever US laws refer or relate to foreign countries, nations, states, or governments, such terms shall include and apply to Taiwan. 

Through this statutory drafting, domestic legislation effectively creates a functional legal fiction: it directs municipal courts and administrative agencies to treat an unrecognized entity as an independent sovereign state for all domestic statutory purposes—including arms transfer authorities, export licensing, and investment protection—while maintaining the executive's diplomatic posture of "strategic ambiguity."

## 3. Unofficial Bilateral Agreements and Treaty Law

The operational execution of relations with unrecognized self-governing entities requires entering into technical and economic pacts—ranging from double-taxation agreements and air transport pacts to supply-chain and investment facilitation frameworks (such as the US–Taiwan Initiative on 21st-Century Trade).

This practice raises procedural questions under the law of treaties:

1. **Capacity Under VCLT Article 6:** Article 6 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties provides that "every State possesses capacity to conclude treaties." When pacts are signed between nominal non-governmental institutes (e.g., AIT and TECRO) rather than official foreign ministries, the agreements fall outside the formal scope of the VCLT.
2. **Binding Character Under Domestic Law:** Because these instruments are executed under domestic statutory authority rather than Article II treaty ratification mechanisms, their domestic enforceability relies entirely on congressional delegation and municipal administrative implementation.
3. **Third-Party Standing Before International Tribunals:** In the World Trade Organization, accession occurs as a "separate customs territory possessing full autonomy in the conduct of its external commercial relations" under GATT Article XII, rather than as a sovereign state. This allows participation in multilateral trade agreements and recourse to dispute settlement without resolving contested underlying sovereignty claims.

---

## Doctrinal Implications for International Order

The endurance of strategic ambiguity reveals the flexibility of modern international law. By decoupling factual governance and commercial capacity from formal diplomatic recognition, domestic legal systems have developed mechanisms to engage with de facto states. 

However, as geopolitical tensions across maritime corridors intensify, the limits of statutory ambiguity become apparent: domestic legislation can direct municipal courts and regulate bilateral trade, but it cannot resolve the unresolved status of contested territories within the formal collective security architecture of the United Nations Charter.
