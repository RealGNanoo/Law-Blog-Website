**SINGAPORE** — Transmitting over 95 percent of intercontinental data traffic, submarine telecommunications cables constitute the foundational physical infrastructure of the global digital economy. As coastal states advance domestic maritime spatial planning, expand marine protected areas, and designate critical infrastructure security corridors, the installation and maintenance of trans-oceanic fiber-optic networks have encountered growing regulatory friction within the 200-nautical-mile Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) and on the continental shelf.

The primary legal controversy centers on the statutory balance struck in the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS): Does the coastal state’s sovereign jurisdiction over continental shelf resources and environmental protection authorize it to mandate prior authorization, impose route alterations, or delay vessel repair permits for international cables transiting its EEZ?

---

## 1. The Statutory Framework: Articles 58, 79, and 112

Under UNCLOS, submarine cables enjoy a specialized dual regime:

* **The High-Seas Freedom Preserved (Article 58(1) & Article 87(1)(c)):** Within the EEZ, all states—coastal or landlocked—enjoy the traditional high-seas freedoms of navigation, overflight, and the **laying of submarine cables and pipelines**, subject to the relevant provisions of the Convention.
* **The Continental Shelf Regime (Article 79(1)):** All states are entitled to lay submarine cables and pipelines on the continental shelf in accordance with the provisions of Article 79. Crucially, **Article 79(2)** establishes the baseline negative obligation: *"Subject to its right to take reasonable measures for the exploration of the continental shelf, the exploitation of its natural resources and the prevention, reduction and control of pollution from pipelines, the coastal State may not impede the laying or maintenance of such cables or pipelines."*
* **Delineation of the Course (Article 79(3)):** The delineation of the course for the laying of pipelines on the continental shelf is subject to the consent of the coastal state. However, by deliberate textual omission, **pipelines** require consent, whereas submarine **telecommunications cables** do not.

The drafting history of UNCLOS Part VI confirms that the conference explicitly rejected proposals to require coastal state consent for the routing of telecommunications cables across the continental shelf, reserving consent mechanisms exclusively for hydrocarbon pipelines and installations directly involved in resource exploitation.

## 2. Coastal State Regulatory Creep: Environmental and Route Approvals

Despite the unambiguous wording of Article 79(2), several coastal states have introduced municipal regulations requiring commercial cable consortia to submit comprehensive Environmental Impact Assessments (EIAs), undergo domestic permitting procedures, and pay administrative fees prior to laying transit cables across the continental shelf.

Coastal authorities justify these domestic regimes under two statutory provisions:

| Statutory Basis | Coastal State Legal Theory | Maritime Cable Operator / Flag State Position |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **UNCLOS Article 79(2)** | Coastal state retains the right to take "reasonable measures" to prevent pollution and protect mineral exploration. | Subsea telecommunications cables are chemically inert, generate negligible benthic disturbance, and do not qualify as pollution. |
| **UNCLOS Article 56(1)(b)(iii)** | Jurisdiction with regard to the "protection and preservation of the marine environment" in the EEZ. | Article 56(2) mandates that coastal states "shall have due regard to the rights and duties of other States"; environmental rules cannot extinguish Article 58 freedoms. |
| **UNCLOS Article 60** | Coastal state exclusive jurisdiction over artificial islands, installations, and structures in the EEZ. | Subsea cables resting on the seabed are physical conduits, not "installations or structures" within the meaning of Article 60. |

Where a coastal state attempts to condition transit cable operations on discretionary domestic permits—or uses permitting delays to advance strategic maritime leverage—it exceeds the "reasonable measures" exception in Article 79(2). 

While a coastal state can legitimately designate coordinates where cable laying would physically obstruct active offshore petroleum platforms, seabed mining sites, or designated marine conservation sanctuaries, it cannot assert a discretionary power to veto or indefinitely delay non-resource infrastructure transiting its maritime zones.

## 3. Maintenance, Repair Operations, and Cabotage Restrictions

The most acute operational bottleneck occurs at the repair stage. Modern fiber-optic cables face frequent physical disruption caused by commercial bottom-trawling nets, dragging anchors, and seismic seabed displacements. Restoring network connectivity requires the immediate deployment of specialized cable-repair ships.

In several jurisdictions, municipal authorities have subjected foreign-flagged cable-repair vessels to domestic **cabotage laws** and lengthy national security security-clearance protocols, barring foreign repair vessels from operating within the EEZ without domestic licenses:

1. **The Breach of Article 79(2):** Article 79(2) explicitly protects the "laying or **maintenance**" of cables. A refusal to issue prompt entry or repair clearances to international repair vessels constitutes an impermissible impediment to cable maintenance under international law.
2. **Freedom of Navigation Under Article 58:** Specialized cable-laying and repair vessels retain freedom of navigation in the EEZ. While engaged in repair work, their operations remain protected as activities associated with the operation of submarine cables under Article 58(1).
3. **The Crime of Damaging Submarine Cables (Article 113):** UNCLOS obligates every state to adopt necessary laws and regulations to ensure that the breaking or injury by a vessel flying its flag of a submarine cable done willfully or through culpable negligence is a punishable offense. In practice, municipal enforcement of Article 113 protections remains weak, leaving undersea links vulnerable to physical damage while repair operations face regulatory delay.

---

## Systemic Trajectory

The legal regime governing submarine telecommunications infrastructure reflects a growing division between physical connectivity and territorial maritime governance. 

While UNCLOS was designed to safeguard the high-seas character of subsea cables against coastal state intervention, municipal assertions of security jurisdiction and environmental licensing are redefining the boundaries of the EEZ. As dependence on cross-border cloud networks and secure data routing deepens, preserving the clear textual limits of Article 79 will remain vital to preventing the jurisdictional enclosure of international waters.
