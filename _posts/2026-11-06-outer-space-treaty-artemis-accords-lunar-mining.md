**VIENNA** — With uncrewed prospecting landers operating at the lunar South Pole and international programs advancing plans for permanent surface infrastructure, the legal regime governing space resource utilization has moved from theoretical jurisprudence to operational regulation. The primary objective is the extraction of in situ resources—specifically water ice trapped within permanently shadowed craters for life support and propellant production, alongside regolith and rare minerals.

However, the rapid development of commercial extraction frameworks has brought long-standing doctrinal ambiguities in international space law into sharp focus. The central controversy divides into two related inquiries: Does the extraction and commercial ownership of celestial resources violate the non-appropriation principle under Article II of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty (OST)? And can operational "safety zones" established around lunar facilities be reconciled with the freedom of access guaranteed under Article I?

---

## 1. Article II of the Outer Space Treaty: The Non-Appropriation Principle

The foundational constraint of international space law is codified in Article II of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty:
> *"Outer space, including the Moon and other celestial bodies, is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or occupation, or by any other means."*

The textual scope of this prohibition is comprehensive: it forbids claims of territorial sovereignty, permanent military occupation, and sovereign boundary delimitation. Furthermore, under Article VI of the OST, states bear international responsibility for national activities in outer space, whether carried out by governmental agencies or non-governmental entities (such as private commercial mining consortia). Consequently, a state cannot circumvent Article II simply by delegating resource claims to private corporations.

The legal debate turns on whether Article II prohibits the appropriation of **subsurface and extracted resources**, or whether it applies strictly to the **celestial body itself (territory)**:

* **The High Seas Analogy (Use and Extraction):** Advanced by the United States, Luxembourg, the United Arab Emirates, Japan, and signatories to the Artemis Accords, this view draws a functional distinction between territorial sovereignty and resource extraction. Analogous to high seas fishing under customary law and UNCLOS Article 87, while no state may claim sovereignty over the ocean (or celestial territory), private entities authorized by their flag state may extract and reduce resources (fish or regolith) into lawful private ownership pursuant to the freedom of scientific exploration and use guaranteed under Article I of the OST.
* **The Prohibitionist / Common Heritage Position:** Advanced by critics of unilateral space mining legislation and states aligned with the 1979 Moon Agreement, this construction holds that natural resources in place form an integral physical component of the celestial body. In the absence of an agreed international regime, unilateral domestic licensing authorizing nationals to possess and sell extracted space resources constitutes appropriation "by any other means" in direct contravention of Article II.

## 2. The Artemis Accords Architecture and Section 10

To establish state practice supporting resource utilization outside the formal United Nations treaty-making machinery, the United States and international partners drafted the **Artemis Accords**—a non-binding plurilateral set of principles for civil space exploration.

Section 10 of the Accords directly addresses the non-appropriation controversy:

| Framework Provision | Treaty / Political Instrument | Legal Mechanism |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Section 10(2) Artemis Accords** | Plurilateral Political Understanding (Non-binding) | Affirmation that the extraction and utilization of space resources does not inherently constitute national appropriation under Article II OST. |
| **Section 11(7) Artemis Accords** | Plurilateral Coordination Mechanism | Establishment of temporary "safety zones" around operational sites to avoid harmful interference under Article IX OST. |
| **Article 11 Moon Agreement (1979)** | Multilateral Treaty (Limited ratification) | Designates the Moon and its resources as the "Common Heritage of Mankind," requiring an international regime to govern exploitation once feasible. |

By securing dozens of state accessions across five continents, the signatories of the Artemis Accords are consciously developing **subsequent state practice** within the meaning of Article 31(3)(b) of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT), seeking to crystallize an interpretation of the OST that treats unilateral resource extraction as legally permissible under customary international law.

Crucially, the 1979 Moon Agreement—which explicitly conditions extraction on an equitable multilateral sharing mechanism similar to deep-seabed mining under UNCLOS Part XI—has been ratified by fewer than twenty states and rejected by all major spacefaring nations, leaving it doctrinally marginal in defining customary norms.

## 3. "Safety Zones" vs. Freedom of Access Under Article I

Beyond ownership of extracted regolith, the operational footprint of mining infrastructure creates severe physical coordination challenges. Under Section 11 of the Artemis Accords, signatories agree to establish **"safety zones"**—geographical areas around an installation where notification and technical coordination are required to avoid harmful interference.

This mechanism intersects directly with core OST disciplines:

1. **Freedom of Scientific Investigation and Access (Article I):** Outer space and celestial bodies must remain "free for exploration and use by all States without discrimination of any kind" and provide "free access to all areas of celestial bodies."
2. **Prevention of Harmful Interference (Article IX):** States must conduct all activities with "due regard to the corresponding interests of all other States Parties" and undertake international consultations prior to any activity that would cause potentially harmful interference with others.

The critical legal dilemma is **temporal and spatial permanence**:
If a safety zone around a lunar water extraction facility covers an entire crater rim—a scarce location of continuous solar illumination and localized water-ice deposits—and endures indefinitely, the zone functions practically as an exclusionary perimeter. 

Proponents emphasize that safety zones are non-exclusionary, temporary coordination tools intended to mitigate physical hazards such as high-velocity dust ejecta generated by rocket landings. However, if third states are effectively prevented from accessing strategically unique lunar sites due to safety perimeter declarations, the mechanism risks amounting to de facto appropriation by "use or occupation" under Article II.

## Doctrinal Outlook in the UN Framework

The unilateral and plurilateral regulation of celestial resources has shifted discussions to the United Nations Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (UN COPUOS), where its specialized Working Group on Legal Aspects of Space Resource Activities continues to deliberate over international governance principles.

While major spacefaring states have effectively foreclosed the adoption of a mandatory benefit-sharing regime analogous to the Moon Agreement, customary international law will not remain settled by plurilateral accords alone. 

As extraction activities move closer to physical execution, the legitimacy of lunar mining will depend on whether spacefaring powers can reconcile domestic private property claims with multilateral transparency, scientific data sharing, and the strict non-appropriation mandates of the Outer Space Treaty.
